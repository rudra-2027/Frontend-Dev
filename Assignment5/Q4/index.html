Mental Models and Making Decisions You Don’t Regret

September 13, 2018| Darius Foroux

What makes a good decision? When I ask people that question, I often get answers like: “When the outcome is successful.” Why is it that we, as a society, romanticize outcomes? Only things and people that succeed are celebrated. But it’s also misleading. We tend to overlook cases that did not come with a successful outcome.

In hindsight, we can all look at mistakes and say they were imminent. But if preventing mistakes is that easy, why do we still make decisions that we regret? Take the case of the Titanic. Looking back, we know it made many costly mistakes. For example, it didn’t carry enough lifeboats or undergo full testing before the voyage.

But here’s the thing—no one wanted the Titanic disaster to happen. And no one predicted it until after the fact. A few decades earlier, Fyodor Dostoevsky famously said:

“Everything seems stupid when it fails.”
It’s easy to look at success and attribute it to good decision making, but failure is not always explained by bad decision making.

Peter Bevelin, the author of Seeking Wisdom, puts it perfectly: “Good decisions can lead to bad outcomes and vice versa.” You can’t predict the future, and sometimes even bad decisions can lead to good outcomes. That’s why teaching how to make “good” decisions is often pointless—what truly matters is the process.

The way you look at how something works in the real world is called a mental model. When we make decisions, we often jump straight to outcomes instead of examining our thinking process. But whatever your reason, skipping the process altogether is the only real way to make bad decisions—regardless of the outcome.

Instead of focusing on success, focus on how comprehensive your decision-making process is. Knowledge without application is useless. That’s why I recommend reading these two books:

  • Seeking Wisdom by Peter Bevelin — mental models of Charlie Munger and Warren Buffett.
  • Fooled By Randomness by Nassim Taleb — understanding how random events influence outcomes.

We can never predict the future, but we can make decisions we don’t regret. As Jim Rohn once said:

“We must all suffer one of two things: the pain of discipline or the pain of regret or disappointment.”
Which type of suffering hurts more? It’s up to you to decide.
